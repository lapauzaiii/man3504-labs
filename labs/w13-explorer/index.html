    Three bottom lines

    Lola June

    Before we pick a supplier, let's agree on what "performing well" means. The Triple Bottom Line says Green Build is measured on People, Planet, and Profit. Some sustainability moves pay for themselves; others cost real money. Let's sort a few.

    Predict first

    Of the four Green Build decisions below, how many do you think both save money and help the environment?

    Explore: which bottom line does each measure inform?

    Explore: aligned, or a real trade-off?

    "Aligned" means it lowers cost and environmental impact together. "Trade-off" means someone has to pay for the sustainability benefit.

    Lola's take

    Two of the four are aligned (LED lighting, full truckloads) and two are trade-offs (recycled steel at a premium, deeper supplier audits). That split is the honest version of Section 1: sustainability is easy when it also cuts cost and hard when it doesn't. Notice that the full-truckload move is the textbook's energy-efficiency domain; freight density lowers cost and emissions per unit together.

    The measures show that the Planet dimension is more than carbon (waste diverted from landfill counts), and that the People dimension reaches past Green Build's own payroll to subcontractor workers. Keep both in mind for the next stop.

    Stop 2 of 5 · W13.A Section 2 · about 7 minutes

    Who can you see?

    Lola June

    Green Build buys windows from an assembler. The assembler buys glass and aluminum, and those suppliers buy smelted metal and mined sand. Green Build only has a contract with the first one. So where does the labor and environmental risk actually sit?

    Predict first

    If Green Build audits only its Tier 1 window assembler, about how much of the supply chain's labor and environmental risk can it see?

    Explore: turn on audits tier by tier

    Illustrative shares of identified risk by tier, with the yearly cost of auditing each tier.

    Risk Green Build can see (solid) versus risk it cannot see (hatched)

    Lola's take

    Auditing Tier 1 alone shows about 15% of the risk for $4,000 a year. Adding Tier 2 gets you to 50% for $16,000. Seeing all of it costs $46,000, and most of that is Tier 3, where Green Build has no contract and the most risk sits. That's the reading's point: ethical exposure is usually invisible from the buyer unless someone actively looks, and Tier 1 is the easy part.

    Audits aren't perfect, either; they can be gamed and certifications vary in rigor. The obligation is transparency and improvement, not a claim of zero risk. And remember the key principle: choosing not to look is itself a process design choice.

    Stop 3 of 5 · W13.A Section 3 · about 10 minutes

    Invoice price vs. total cost of ownership

    Lola June

    Three quotes for the same window. Vietnam is cheapest on the invoice by a wide margin. But a window that arrives cracked, or late, or that forces you to hold a month of extra stock, costs more than its price tag. Let's add it all up.

    Predict first

    Invoice prices are $310 (Vietnam), $345 (Mexico), and $372 (Georgia). Which supplier has the lowest total cost per window?

    Explore: build the TCO

    Safety stock follows the same policy as the reading: 2 weeks of supply if lead time is 6 weeks or less, 4 weeks if longer. Expected disruption cost = disruptions per year × cost per disruption ÷ 4,000 units.

    Price Freight and duty Inspection Defects Safety stock Expected disruption

    Lola's take

    At the starting settings, Monterrey wins at $379.94 per window, Vietnam is $385.35, and Georgia is $392.86. Vietnam's $35 invoice advantage over Mexico disappears into defects ($10.85), extra safety stock for a 12-week lead time ($5.25), freight ($42), and expected disruptions ($11.25). Over 4,000 windows, Vietnam costs about $21,600 a year more than Mexico.

    Drag Vietnam's disruptions below about 0.78 a year and it wins again, so the answer depends on how much you trust that risk estimate. A 10% tariff pushes Vietnam to almost $418. And notice that reshoring isn't automatically best: Georgia has the shortest lead time and fewest defects, but it still costs about $51,700 a year more than Monterrey. That's nearshoring's case in one picture.

    Stop 4 of 5 · W13.A Sections 3–4 · about 7 minutes

    Carbon and revenue at risk

    Lola June

    TCO covers costs. It doesn't cover the clients who pay Green Build a premium because it can prove where its materials come from. Vietnam can't provide that proof; its plant won't allow third-party audits. Let's see what that puts at stake, and what each choice does to carbon.

    Predict first

    If Green Build switches to the unverified Vietnam supplier and loses its low-carbon premium, how does the revenue at risk compare with Vietnam's TCO gap of about $21,600 a year?

    Explore: the full picture

    Revenue at risk = share of revenue tied to the premium × total revenue × premium. Annual CO₂ = kg per window × 4,000 windows.

    Each supplier's shipping and manufacturing footprint: Vietnam 95 kg, Mexico 40 kg, Georgia 22 kg CO₂ per window.

    Lola's take

    At 35% of $9.6 million with a 6% premium, $201,600 a year rides on being able to verify sourcing. That's about nine times Vietnam's TCO gap. Like the TCO itself, it's an estimate, and it only becomes a loss if clients act on it. But it belongs in the decision, and it would never appear in a cost-only comparison.

    Now the hard part. Georgia emits 72,000 kg less CO₂ a year than Monterrey, but costs about $51,700 more, roughly $0.72 per kilogram avoided. Is that worth it? Operations can't answer that alone; it's a strategy call. This is Section 4's point: the earlier tools you learned (TCO, safety stock, defect cost) are how you put numbers on the trade-off so leaders can make it explicitly.

    Stop 5 of 5 · W13.A Section 5 · about 8 minutes

    Make it a project

    Lola June

    Green Build chooses Monterrey. "Switch suppliers" sounds like one decision, but it's really a project. Let's build its Work Breakdown Structure, and follow the 100% Rule: everything in scope goes in, nothing out of scope sneaks in.

    Predict first

    Where does "give the current Vietnam supplier six months' written notice" belong?

    Explore: assign each work item to a Level 1 phase, or mark it out of scope

    Level 0 is the project goal: Transition Green Build's window supply to a verified nearshore supplier.

    Lola's take

    Notice to the outgoing supplier is in scope. It belongs under Contract and legal. A responsible exit is part of the work; if it isn't a work package with an owner, it tends not to happen. The same goes for mapping the Tier 2 sources: that's Stop 2's ethics turned into something someone actually has to do by a date.

    The hospital bid and the logo redesign might be worthwhile, but they belong to other projects. Putting them here is scope creep, the most common reason projects run over. A WBS is a planning tool for temporary, unique work; a process map is for the ongoing work that follows once Monterrey is running.

    The windows are ordered, the project has a plan, and I have inspected the new delivery dock thoroughly. Thanks for walking the site with me.

    Five ideas to carry forward

    1. Performance has three bottom lines. Some sustainability moves pay for themselves; others are real trade-offs that someone must choose deliberately.
    2. Most supply chain risk sits where you have no contract. Tier 1 is the easy part to see; choosing not to look is a process design choice.
    3. The cheapest invoice is often not the cheapest supplier. Defects, safety stock, freight, and disruptions belong in the TCO.
    4. Some stakes sit outside TCO. Revenue at risk and carbon need their own numbers, so leaders can make the trade-off explicitly.
    5. A sourcing change is a project. A WBS that follows the 100% Rule turns commitments, including a responsible exit, into owned work.

Building on W2 & W3: In Week 2 you mapped process flows and identified where work had to wait. In Week 3, the Theory of Constraints said the bottleneck determines system throughput. CPM is the project-level version of that same logic: the critical path is the bottleneck of the project — it determines minimum completion time, and any delay on it propagates directly to the project finish date.

Phase 3 — Guided Diagnostic

Step-by-step frameworks for each problem type you'll encounter on W6.C and W6.D.

10–15 min
Framework A — Aggregate Planning Cost Comparison
1
Convert demand to workforce Divide each period's demand by output per worker. Round up — you can't have a fraction of a worker. Write the required workforce for each period.
2
Calculate period-to-period changes Compare each period's workforce to the prior period. Positive delta = hires; negative delta = layoffs. First period compares to current workforce.
3
Multiply changes by their unit costs Hires × hiring cost per worker + Layoffs × layoff cost per worker = chase strategy total.
4
For level strategy: track ending inventory Each period: Ending Inv = Prior Ending Inv + Production − Demand. If any period goes negative, the plan is infeasible as written.
5
Sum holding costs Multiply each period's ending inventory by the holding cost per unit. Sum across all periods. Compare to chase cost — the lower number wins unless other constraints (shelf life, labor relations) disqualify it.
✅
Prior-week connection (W5): The demand figures you're planning against came from a forecast. A higher MAD signals greater forecast uncertainty and may justify more inventory, capacity flexibility, or another buffer after considering service and cost trade-offs.
Framework B — Job Sequencing
1
Sort by the chosen rule SPT: ascending processing time. EDD: ascending due date. FCFS: arrival order. CR: ascending (time remaining ÷ work remaining); recalculate after each completed job.
2
Compute cumulative completion times Job 1 completes at its own processing time. Each subsequent job completes at the prior cumulative time + its own processing time.
3
Calculate lateness for each job Lateness = Completion Time − Due Date. If this is negative (early), record 0. Never record a negative lateness.
4
Average flow time Sum all completion times, divide by number of jobs. Lower is better for throughput and WIP.
5
Make the business judgment SPT minimizes average flow time but may let a high-value job run late. EDD minimizes maximum lateness. Choose the rule that protects what your specific business values most.
⚠️
Prior-week connection (W3 TOC): The sequencing rule only matters at the bottleneck. Non-bottleneck workstations have slack; you can't optimize the whole system by sequencing a non-constraint. Ask: is this the bottleneck? before applying a priority rule.
Framework C — MRP Net Requirements
1
Identify gross requirements from the MPS × BOM Gross requirement = MPS quantity × number of that component per finished unit (from the bill of materials).
2
Identify all available supply Available = On-hand inventory + Scheduled receipts arriving before the requirement is needed. Subtract nothing yet.
3
Set the desired ending safety stock Safety stock is the inventory you want remaining after the gross requirement is satisfied. It is part of the inventory target, not an amount added automatically after every calculation.
4
Calculate the net requirement Net Req = max(0, Gross Req + Safety Stock − Available). Under lot-for-lot ordering, the planned receipt equals this net requirement.
5
Offset for lead time Planned Order Release Period = Requirement Period − Lead Time. If the requirement is in Week 4 and lead time is 2 weeks, the order goes in Week 2. Subtracting lead time, never adding it.
🚫
Most common error: Students add the lead time instead of subtracting it. The order release goes before the requirement, not after. If the requirement is Week 5 and lead time is 2 weeks → release Week 3, not Week 7.
Framework D — CPM Forward & Backward Pass
1
Forward pass — compute ES and EF for every activity Start at the beginning: ES of the first activity = 0. EF = ES + Duration. For any activity with multiple predecessors, ES = max(EF of all predecessors).
2
Project duration The project's earliest completion = max(EF of all final activities). This is the critical path length.
3
Backward pass — compute LF and LS Start at the end: LF of the last activity = Project Duration. LS = LF − Duration. For any activity with multiple successors, LF = min(LS of all successors).
4
Calculate slack Slack = LS − ES (equivalently: LF − EF). Zero slack = critical path. Positive slack = the activity can be delayed that many periods without affecting project completion.
5
Crashing decision Only crash critical-path activities — crashing a non-critical activity wastes money without shortening the project. If two paths tie for critical after a crash, both must be crashed simultaneously to compress further.
✅
Prior-week connection (W4 Quality): In Week 4 you saw that rework is one of the most expensive forms of waste (cost of poor quality). Crashing an activity may introduce quality risk if added resources, handoffs, or time pressure are poorly managed. CPM shows the schedule impact; quality risk requires a separate managerial judgment.

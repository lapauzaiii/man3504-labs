    Lower the water

    Lola June

    Cowford keeps about two weeks of finished beer in the cold room, "just in case." The reading says inventory is like water hiding rocks in a river. Let's drain the cold room a little at a time and see what's underneath.

    Predict first

    Lola's take

    Watch the order of events. The cushion came down, a problem surfaced, and the shortages stopped only after the team fixed the cause. That loop is kaizen: lower the water, find the rock, remove it, then lower the water again.

    Cutting inventory without fixing what surfaces just turns hidden waste into empty taps. That's the "Where lean gets hard" warning in Section 1: in some operations, customers hit the rock before you can remove it, so you lower the water in steps you can actually keep up with.

    Wastes travel in packs. Brewing to last year's forecast (overproduction) left kegs in the cold room (inventory) that a forklift keeps hauling around (transportation). Fix the cause and several symptoms go with it.

    And one of the longest waits on the floor, four weeks of lagering, wasn't waste. The beer is changing the whole time, and customers pay for that. The test is always "does the customer value this?", not "is anything moving?" Hold onto that; it comes back at Stop 6.

    Push vs. pull

    Lola June

    Cowford sells canned IPA in the taproom and to two distributors. Below are ten days of demand. On the left, packaging runs a fixed forecast every day (push). On the right, packaging only refills what customers actually took (pull).

    Predict first

    If the forecast turns out to be wrong, which approach suffers more?

    Explore

    Canned IPA demand (cases per day)

    Push: package to the forecast

    Pull: refill only what was sold

    Demand Cases on the shelf at opening Sales lost

    Pull refill rule: each day's sales are packaged the next day and are back on the shelf the morning after. Push packaging also lands the next morning.

    Lola's take

    Push only looked good when the forecast was right. The optimistic forecast left 177 cases sitting in the cold room by day 10; the pessimistic one lost 81 sales. Pull never used the forecast at all. Its results depended on one decision: how many cases you authorized.

    At 80 cases, pull couldn't cover the refill gap and lost more sales than the pessimistic push. At 140, it never missed. So pull isn't magic. It replaces "how accurate is our forecast?" with "how big should our kanban be?", and that is exactly the question the kanban formula at Stop 7 answers: enough to cover demand while containers wait and get refilled, plus a buffer.

    Stop 4 of 7 · W10.A Section 3 · about 7 minutes

    Takt and heijunka on the canning line

    Lola June

    Takt time is the drumbeat the customer sets. Pick the shift length and how many cases distributors want, then watch which canning stations can keep the beat. After that, we'll plan the whole week.

    Predict first

    Distributor orders jump from 600 to 700 cases per shift. What happens on the line?

    Explore: takt time

    Takt time: 45.0 seconds per case

    Explore: leveling the week (heijunka)

    Weekly demand: 1,500 cases of IPA, 900 of Lager, and 600 of the Seasonal. The line can can 600 cases a day.

    Assumes each beer sells evenly through the week, and that anything canned today can ship today.

    Lola's take

    More demand in the same shift means shorter takt, about 38.6 seconds at 700 cases. The filler (44 s) and seamer (40 s) can't keep that beat no matter how hard the crew works; they need a design change, such as a second head, a faster changeover, or a longer shift.

    Leveling the week means every beer is made every day, so finished beer stops piling up and distributors never wait days for the Lager. Malt and hops use also becomes steady, which suppliers love. The price is changeovers: 14 a week instead of 2. At 45 minutes each, that's 630 minutes, more than a full shift. At an 8-minute setup, it's 112. That's why heijunka and single-digit setup always travel together.

    Explore

    Which cell fits?

    The taproom to-go counter. One bartender runs a small can filler, a seamer, and a label printer for crowlers. Each machine runs on its own for about 40 seconds once loaded.

    Packaging for the IPA family. 12-ounce cans, 16-ounce cans, and sixth-barrel kegs share filling, quality checks, and palletizing. Three people staff the area.

    Lola's take

    No machine got faster. What changed was everything between the machines: the trip shrank from about 640 feet to 140, and the places where beer waits in a queue dropped from four to one. That's transportation and waiting waste from Stop 2, removed by layout rather than effort.

    The two fit questions turn on the textbook distinction: an OWMM cell is one worker tending several automatic machines for one product; a GT cell groups dissimilar machines for a family of similar products, staffed by several people. And remember Section 4's warning: the right layout is often known for years before anyone pays for the downtime to move the equipment.

    Lola's take

    Same beer, same hours, and PCE runs from under 1% to about 80% depending only on how you classify fermentation and lagering. Neither number is "the answer." What matters is stating your convention and using it consistently, because a high PCE can hide real waste.

    Either way, the improvement target is identical: 246 hours of plain waiting. Removing the avoidable waits cut lead time by 158 hours without touching the beer. And the canning line's 3 idle hours are resource idle time, any period a ready and available operator or machine sits inactive because of delays, imbalances, or shortages. It's real waste for the line, but the beer wasn't waiting during it, so it doesn't belong in product lead time.

    Kanban and the malt supplier

    Lola June

    Malt comes in pallets of four sacks, and the brewhouse uses about 12 sacks a day. How many pallets should the brewery authorize? It depends on the supplier relationship. And what happens when a truck is late?

    Predict first

    Cowford switches from weekly orders to frequent small deliveries from one partner supplier. What happens?

    Explore

    Now the truck is late

    Brewhouse days lost: 0.5

    Lola's take

    Moving from weekly orders to a partner supplier cut authorized malt from 12 pallets to 5, because the wait time v in the kanban formula fell from 3 days to 1. But the spare cover beyond normal replenishment fell too, so a truck that's a day late now idles the brewhouse longer. Lean vendor relationships trade inventory for dependence.

    The mitigations are the ones you just tried: raise a for critical inputs, qualify a backup, and share forecasts so the supplier sees trouble coming. JIT II goes further than VMI generally: the supplier's planner works inside the brewery. That lowers inventory again and spots problems earlier, but it doesn't remove the need for a contingency.

    Seven ideas to carry forward

    1. Inventory hides problems. Lower it in steps and fix what surfaces, or you just trade waste for shortages.
    2. Wastes cause each other. Find the cause (often overproduction), and some waits aren't waste at all.
    3. Pull replaces forecast accuracy with kanban sizing. It still needs enough authorized stock to cover the refill gap.
    4. A station slower than takt is a bottleneck, and leveling the schedule only pays off when setups are short.
    5. Layout removes transportation and waiting that no amount of machine speed can.
    6. PCE depends on classification. State your convention, and keep resource idle time out of product lead time.
    7. Lean vendor relationships trade inventory for dependence. Plan the contingency before you need it.

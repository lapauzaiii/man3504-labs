The big picture

Every topic answered the same question

How does an organization design and run the system that delivers value, reliably, efficiently and responsibly?

Forecast demand→Set capacity→Plan and schedule→Design the supply chain→Hold inventory→Make it flow→Build in quality→Control the process→Source responsibly→Measure and improve
What we said

A bad forecast creates the wrong capacity, which creates the wrong inventory, which hides problems lean would expose, which shows up as quality failures, which the scorecard eventually catches in the financials. On the exam, ask yourself where in this chain each question lives, and the right tool usually follows.

Quick-fire · 8 minutes

Which tool would you reach for?

Cowford Coffee wants to know how many bags of beans to order each time.

EOQ (Week 9), then check it against shelf life.

Cowford Bakery's oven line can't keep up with customer demand, but nobody agrees on why.

Compare each step's cycle time with takt time; find the bottleneck (Weeks 2–3, 10).

Cowford Logistics wants to know whether late deliveries rose last week or just bounced around.

A p-chart with run rules (Week 12).

Cowford Medical Supply wants to know which defect type to attack first.

A Pareto chart (Week 11).

Cowford Brewery has a cheaper malt supplier with longer lead times and no audits.

Total cost of ownership plus a supplier-risk and ethics review (Week 13).

Cowford Fitness's revenue is fine, but instructor turnover has doubled.

A Balanced Scorecard reading: Learning & Growth is a leading indicator (Week 14).

Cowford Green Build needs to know if a 16-week rollout deadline is realistic.

CPM for the critical path, PERT for the probability (Weeks 6, 14).

Cowford Tech Services' help-desk forecast keeps coming in under actual demand.

Check the tracking signal for bias, then reconsider the method or α (Week 4).

What we said

Name the tool before you calculate anything. Many exam questions are really "which tool applies here?" with the math as a second step.

Practice round 1 · Weeks 1–6

Forecasting and capacity

A. Cowford Tech Services had 410, 450 and 470 help-desk tickets in the last three weeks. What is the three-week moving average forecast for next week?

(410 + 450 + 470) ÷ 3 = 443.3 tickets.

B. A Cowford Green Build prefab line has three steps of 50, 75 and 40 seconds per panel, one worker each. Maximum panels per hour?

The 75-second step is the bottleneck: 3,600 ÷ 75 = 48 panels per hour.

What we said

For capacity, the slowest step sets the pace, not the sum of the steps. For forecasting, know moving averages, exponential smoothing, MAD, and the tracking signal (CFE ÷ MAD; beyond ±4 means bias).

Practice round 2 · Weeks 8–10

Supply chains, inventory and lean

A. Cowford Coffee uses 5,000 bags of cups a year; each order costs $40 and holding a bag for a year costs $1.60. EOQ?

√(2 × 5,000 × 40 ÷ 1.60) = √250,000 = 500 bags.

B. A Cowford Bakery line has 480 minutes a day and demand of 1,200 rolls. Takt time?

28,800 seconds ÷ 1,200 = 24 seconds per roll.

C. Cowford Logistics holds $120,000 of inventory; annual cost of goods sold is $3,120,000. Weeks of supply?

Weekly COGS = $60,000; $120,000 ÷ $60,000 = 2 weeks.

What we said

Watch units: takt in seconds versus minutes, lead time in days versus weeks. Safety stock uses √L, not L. Kanban containers always round up.

Practice round 3 · Weeks 11–12

Quality and SPC

A. Cowford Green Build inspections average 16 defects per unit. c-chart limits?

16 ± 3√16 = 16 ± 12: UCL = 28, LCL = 4.

B. A part's specification is 4.0 to 6.0 mm, σ = 0.25 mm, and the mean is 5.4 mm. Cp, Cpk, and what to do (critical value 1.33)?

Cp = 2.0 ÷ 1.5 = 1.33 (passes). Cpk = min(1.4 ÷ 0.75, 0.6 ÷ 0.75) = 0.80 (fails). Spread is fine, so recenter toward 5.0 mm.

What we said

Control limits come from the process; specification limits come from the customer. In control does not mean capable. Cp fails: reduce variation. Cp passes but Cpk fails: recenter.

Practice round 4 · Weeks 13–14

Responsible sourcing and improvement

A. Cowford Bakery earns $1.5 million a year; 30% comes from products with a 5% "locally milled" premium. Revenue at risk if the claim ends?

0.30 × $1,500,000 × 0.05 = $22,500.

B. An activity has a = 3, m = 6, b = 15 weeks. PERT expected time and variance?

te = (3 + 24 + 15) ÷ 6 = 7.0 weeks. σ² = (12 ÷ 6)² = 4.0.

What we said

TCO adds freight, defects, safety stock and expected disruptions to price. Revenue at risk sits outside TCO. On the scorecard, Learning & Growth leads and Financial lags.

Discussion · 10 minutes

When the math is right and the decision is wrong

EOQ says order 2,000 packs of fresh tortillas; they go stale in 4 days.

EOQ assumes inventory keeps. Cap the order at what can be used before it expires.

The cheaper supplier saves 6% on the invoice price.

Defects, freight and disruptions can reverse the comparison. Compare TCO.

A manager adjusts the process after every below-average reading.

That's tampering. Adjusting a stable process adds variation.

Benchmark price against a self-serve competitor.

A different business model is a category error. Pick comparable benchmarks, and use reverse benchmarking to protect what customers value.

What we said

Several exam questions are built this way: the calculation is fine, and the question is whether the assumptions fit the situation.

Strategy

On exam day

  1. Read every choice before picking one. Distractors are built from common mistakes.
  2. Write the formula first, then plug in numbers.
  3. Check units and rounding: seconds vs. minutes, √L, round kanban up, LCL below zero becomes 0.
  4. On multiple-answer questions, judge each option separately.
  5. If stuck, name the tool first (slide 4), then come back.

Before you start

  • Run the Honorlock system check early
  • Confirm your window, attempts and allowed materials in Canvas
  • Email me with questions before the exam window opens
What we said

Hook · Cowford Bakery

Four loaves just averaged 806 grams. The target is 800. What do you do?

Cowford Bakery's sourdough line pulls four loaves every hour. The latest four averaged 6 grams heavy. The shift lead has the dough divider control in hand.

Commit to an answer. We will come back to it once we have a way to tell the difference.

What we discussed

Start with the customer: a light loaf shortchanges them on a labeled weight, and a heavy loaf gives away margin. Then ask what the manager actually controls. The honest answer to the poll is "it depends on whether 806 is unusual for this process." One number cannot tell you that. You need to know how much the line normally moves. That is what a control chart supplies.

W12.A Section 1

Every process varies. The question is which kind of variation you are looking at.

Common cause

Many small, everyday sources that are built into the current system, such as flour moisture, room humidity, or small hand differences. No single cause dominates.

Manager's move: leave the process alone day to day. Reducing it means redesigning the system.

Special (assignable) cause

A specific event outside normal operation, such as a worn part, a new flour lot, an untrained operator, or a scale that was never re-zeroed.

Manager's move: find it, fix it, and keep it from coming back.

What we discussed

The distinction matters because it tells the manager who should act. Common-cause variation belongs to management, because only a system change will reduce it. Special-cause variation is something the people closest to the work can usually find and fix. If you treat one as the other, you waste money either way.

W12.A Section 1 · Deming's funnel

What happens if we "fix" a stable process after every loaf?

Both runs below use the same natural loaf-to-loaf noise. The only difference is the operator's policy. Predict first: will adjusting after every loaf make the spread smaller, the same, or larger?

Run it to compare the two policies.

What we discussed

Adjusting after every loaf feels responsible, but each adjustment reacts to noise that was never going to repeat. Over many runs, that adds about 40% more spread (the standard deviation grows by roughly the square root of 2). This is what W12.A calls tampering, and it is why a stable process should be left alone until a chart gives a real signal.

W12.A Section 2

Two charts, two questions

X-bar chart: has the center moved?

Plots each subgroup's mean. Centerline = grand mean X̄̄.

UCL, LCL = X̄̄ ± A₂ R̄

R chart: has the spread changed?

Plots each subgroup's range (largest minus smallest). Centerline = average range R̄.

UCL = D₄ R̄   LCL = D₃ R̄

Read the R chart first. If the spread is out of control, the X-bar limits, which are built from R̄, cannot be trusted.

Worked mini-example from W12.A (predict, then open: is it in control?)

Four subgroups of four espresso shots (oz). Subgroup means 2.010, 2.060, 1.975, 2.015; ranges 0.04, 0.04, 0.03, 0.03.

  • X̄̄ = (2.010 + 2.060 + 1.975 + 2.015) / 4 = 2.015 oz
  • R̄ = (0.04 + 0.04 + 0.03 + 0.03) / 4 = 0.035 oz
  • X-bar limits = 2.015 ± 0.729 × 0.035 = 1.989 to 2.041 oz
  • R chart limits = 0 to 0.080 oz (2.282 × 0.035)

Now compare each point with its limits. The R chart is fine: the largest range, 0.04, is well under 0.080. But Subgroup 2's mean (2.060) is above the UCL, and Subgroup 3's mean (1.975) is below the LCL. The spread inside each subgroup is normal, yet the center jumps around, which is exactly the situation the X-bar chart exists to catch. With only four subgroups these are trial limits, so the next step is to investigate those two hours, not to adjust the machine.

What we discussed

A subgroup is a handful of units made close together in time, so the variation inside it is the process's short-term noise. The R chart watches that noise. The X-bar chart asks whether the subgroup averages wander more than that noise would explain. Control limits come from the process's own data. They are not targets or specifications.

What we discussed

Counting backward is an assignable cause: we know exactly what changed and when. Mental load usually makes time feel shorter, so many people overshoot. That shows up as a shift in the X-bar chart, and often as a wider R chart, because people react to the distraction differently. Whatever your class saw, the logic is the point. Limits come from a stable baseline, and new data is judged against them. A signal sends you looking for the cause, not reaching for the controls.

W12.A Section 3

Not just "outside the lines": the Western Electric rules

1

One point beyond 3σ. Investigate now.

2

Eight in a row on one side of the centerline. The mean has shifted and stayed shifted.

3

Six in a row trending up or down. Something is drifting, such as wear, temperature, or fatigue.

4

Two of three beyond 2σ on the same side. An early warning before a limit is crossed.

Each pattern is unlikely to happen by chance in a stable process. More rules catch more problems, but they also cause more false alarms.

What we discussed

The page checks all four rules against our X-bar chart automatically. Look back at the Round 2 result: which rule fired, and would you have caught it by eye? Also note the reverse warning from W12.A. A chart where every point hugs the centerline can mean someone is recording "expected" values instead of real ones.

W12.A Section 2 · Week 11 refresher

"In control" is not the same as "good enough"

Control limits describe what the process does. Specification limits describe what the customer requires. Move the sliders for an illustrative loaf line with a 790 to 810 gram specification.

What we discussed

A process can be perfectly stable and still make out-of-spec loaves, if it is centered in the wrong place or spread too wide. Cp compares the specification width with the process spread. Cpk does the same but also penalizes off-centering, so Cpk ≤ Cp, and the gap between them measures how far off-center the process is. Check stability first; capability numbers from an unstable process can mislead.

W12.A Section 5

Where SPC earns its keep at Cowford, and where it does not

SPC adds value when a process is repetitive, its output is measurable, and it is stable enough to have a baseline. Open each card and decide before you read the note.

What we discussed

Repetitive production such as Bakery, Brewery, Coffee, and Medical Supply fits X-bar and R charts well. Pass/fail service measures such as on-time delivery or class cancellations fit a p-chart. One-of-a-kind work such as a Green Build project has no repeating process to chart. The skill is knowing where the tool applies cleanly, where it applies differently, and where it does not apply at all.
